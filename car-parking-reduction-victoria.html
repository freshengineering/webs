Car parking reductions in Victoria: what council needs to see

A plain guide to Clause 52.06 after VC277, reductions and demand assessments

Get a quote Call 0492 930 492

Can you provide fewer car parks than the planning scheme asks for?

Often, yes. Every planning scheme in Victoria includes Clause 52.06, which sets how many car parking spaces a use must provide. Where a development can't or shouldn't provide that many, you can apply for a permit to reduce the requirement, even to zero. This is called a car parking reduction. You may also hear it called a parking waiver or dispensation.

Council won't approve a reduction on the applicant's word alone. It needs evidence that the parking you provide will meet the real demand, or that any shortfall won't cause problems in surrounding streets. That evidence usually comes as a car parking demand assessment.

This guide explains how the requirement is worked out, what council looks for, and how to give a reduction the best chance.

The short answer

  • Work out the statutory rate first
  • Check the Car Parking Requirement map category
  • Apply for a reduction under Clause 52.06-3
  • Back it with a car parking demand assessment
  • Base it on evidence, not opinion

How the parking requirement is worked out under Clause 52.06

Clause 52.06 applies when a new use starts, or when the floor area or site area of an existing use increases. It also applies when an existing use is intensified, for example by adding patrons or staff. The number of spaces required comes from four things.

  • The rate for the use: a table in Clause 52.06 sets rates for common uses, such as dwellings, offices, shops and restaurants. Each rate is tied to a measure like floor area, bedrooms or patron numbers, so the land use term in your application matters.
  • The site's Car Parking Requirement map category: since Amendment VC277 on 18 December 2025, Clause 52.06 sets rates by location. The Department of Transport and Planning's Car Parking Requirement maps put land in one of four categories based on access to public transport. Categories 1 and 2 have minimum rates only. Category 3 has a minimum and a maximum. Category 4 has no minimum, and a maximum for most uses. Where a site falls in more than one category, the higher category applies to the whole site. To check a site's category, look up the address in VicPlan, the state government's free planning map, and turn on the Car Parking Requirement layer.
  • Parking Overlay: some activity centres have a Parking Overlay whose schedule sets its own rates, or a maximum, in place of the standard table. Existing overlay schedules continue to apply until they are updated, and some Parking Overlays still use the former Column B rates. Check the planning scheme maps for the site.
  • Uses not in the table: where a use isn't listed, parking must be provided to council's satisfaction. A demand assessment is often the clearest way to show what's needed.

Get this step right before anything else. A reduction is measured against the statutory figure, so an error here flows through the whole application. Transitional rules let applications lodged within 180 days after VC277 started rely on whichever of the old or new minimum was lower. That period ended on 16 June 2026, so new applications are assessed under the VC277 rates. Amendment VC311 made further changes to Clause 52.06 on 9 June 2026, mainly to how Parking Overlays work with the new table.

When you need a permit to reduce parking

Clause 52.06-3 requires a permit to provide fewer spaces than the minimum, including reducing it to zero. In Category 3 and 4 areas, you also need a permit to provide more than the maximum. Common situations where a reduction is sought include:

  • A change of use in an existing building with little or no on-site parking
  • An extension that adds floor area on a site with no room for more spaces
  • Mixed-use sites where the uses peak at different times and can share spaces
  • Sites close to train, tram or bus services, or in walkable centres
  • Small lots where on-site parking isn't practical, or would harm the streetscape

The reduction is applied for as part of the planning permit application for the use or development, and it's assessed along with the rest of the application.

When you don't need a permit

Clause 52.06-3 also sets out cases where no permit is needed to provide fewer spaces. The two most common are:

  • The new use needs no more parking than the current use, and no existing spaces are removed
  • The site is in a commercial zone, such as a shopping strip, the new use goes into an existing building without adding floor area, and the shortfall is 10 spaces or fewer

The commercial zone exemption doesn't apply where a Parking Overlay lets you pay instead of providing spaces. Where a permit is needed for a shortfall of 10 spaces or fewer, it can go through VicSmart, the state's faster permit process. If parking is the only permit needed, the application doesn't go out for public notice.

What council looks for in a car parking demand assessment

Clause 52.06 lists the matters a car parking demand assessment should address. They come down to how many vehicles the use will actually attract, when, and for how long. Depending on the use, that includes:

  • The likely number of staff, residents and visitors, and how long they stay
  • How demand changes through the day and week, including short-stay and long-stay parking
  • How efficiently spaces can be shared between uses on the site
  • Any existing shortfall or surplus of parking associated with the current use
  • Access to public transport, and how convenient it is to walk and ride to the site
  • Bicycle parking and end-of-trip facilities
  • Expected car ownership for residential developments
  • Surveys or case studies of comparable sites

Not every factor applies to every use. A strong assessment focuses on the ones that drive demand for the use in question and backs them with data.

Clause 52.06 decision guidelines: what else council weighs up

Even with a good demand assessment, council decides the reduction against the decision guidelines in Clause 52.06. These include:

  • Parking available nearby, including on-street parking and its time limits
  • The effect of fewer spaces on local amenity, including for pedestrians and nearby residents
  • Access to public transport and other ways of getting to the site
  • Whether providing parking on the site is practical, particularly on small lots
  • Local traffic management around the site
  • Any relevant local policy or parking precinct plan

This is why on-street parking surveys often matter as much as the demand figures. If nearby streets are full at the times the use peaks, council will want to know where the extra cars will go.

How to give a reduction the best chance

  • Check the use definition: the rate follows the land use term in the planning scheme, so make sure the application describes the use correctly.
  • Survey comparable sites: data from similar uses in similar locations is stronger than a general argument. Survey on representative days and avoid school and public holidays.
  • Count on-street parking at the peak: record supply and occupancy in nearby streets at the times the use is busiest.
  • Account for the existing use: where the current use already has a shortfall, the assessment can take it into account.
  • Get the layout right: council may not accept spaces that don't meet the design standards in Clause 52.06 and AS 2890.1. Before lodging, check the parking layout against AS 2890.1, including bay sizes, aisle widths, ramps and accessways.
  • Talk to council early: many councils offer pre-application meetings. Raising a proposed reduction there tells you what evidence council's traffic engineer will expect.
  • Lodge the parking case with the application: a complete report at lodgement helps avoid a request for further information later.

If council says no

If council refuses the application, or imposes a parking condition you don't accept, you can usually apply to the Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (VCAT) for a review. VCAT considers the same Clause 52.06 decision guidelines, so the quality of the evidence still carries a lot of weight.

Council's grounds for refusal show what it found lacking. They are the place to start when working out what further evidence is needed.

Frequently asked questions

Can council reduce the car parking requirement to zero?

Yes. Clause 52.06-3 allows the responsible authority to reduce the requirement, including to zero, where the reduction is justified. Council needs evidence that the reduced provision will work, usually a car parking demand assessment, and weighs it against the decision guidelines in the clause.

Do I need a permit to provide less parking?

Usually. Clause 52.06-3 requires a permit to provide fewer spaces than the minimum, including none at all. In Category 3 and 4 areas, you also need a permit to provide more than the maximum. There are exemptions, for example where the new use needs no more parking than the current one, or for a shortfall of 10 spaces or fewer in an existing building in a commercial zone.

What are the Car Parking Requirement maps?

They are maps prepared by the Department of Transport and Planning that sort land into four categories based on access to public transport. Since Amendment VC277 on 18 December 2025, the category sets which Clause 52.06 rates apply to a site, and whether there is a minimum, a maximum or both. They replaced the earlier Column A and Column B rates, which were based on the Principal Public Transport Network Area. Some Parking Overlays still use the former Column B rates. Checking the category first can reduce the requirement before you need to ask for a reduction.

Do I need a demand assessment for a change of use?

Often. When a new use starts in an existing building, the Clause 52.06 requirement for the new use applies. If the site can't provide that many spaces, you'll need a permit to reduce the requirement, and council will expect a car parking demand assessment with the application. The assessment can take account of any shortfall already associated with the current use.

Who prepares a car parking demand assessment?

Usually a traffic engineer. Council's traffic engineer will normally review it, so it needs to show its data and assumptions clearly. Fresh Engineering prepares car parking demand assessments for developments across Melbourne and regional Victoria.

What if council refuses the reduction?

You can usually apply to VCAT to review council's decision, including a refusal or a permit condition you don't accept. VCAT considers the same Clause 52.06 decision guidelines, so the strength of the demand evidence still matters.
