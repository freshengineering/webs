A plain guide to permit triggers, DTP referral and report scope

Does your planning application need a traffic report?

Is a traffic impact assessment (TIA) required for your planning permit? Often, but not always. You need one when the planning scheme, the council or the Department of Transport and Planning (DTP) wants evidence that the development's access, parking and traffic will work safely.

Whether you need one depends on three things: what the planning scheme triggers, how much traffic the use adds, and what council asks for. This guide explains how that usually plays out in Victoria. When a traffic impact assessment is required in Victoria

No single rule covers every application. In practice, a TIA is most often needed in the situations below.

  • New or changed access to a main road: Clause 52.29 of the planning scheme requires a permit to create or alter access to a road in a Transport Zone 2. These are the main roads that DTP manages. The application is referred to DTP, which will expect the access and its traffic impacts to be assessed.
  • Uses that generate a lot of traffic: service stations, supermarkets, childcare centres, drive-throughs and larger residential or industrial developments add trips, turning movements and queues that council will want assessed.
  • Parking that doesn't meet Clause 52.06: you need a permit to provide fewer spaces than the minimum, or more than the maximum in Category 3 and 4 areas. Council will expect a car parking demand assessment, often as part of the traffic report. See our guide to car parking reductions in Victoria.
  • Larger developments: under Clause 66.02-11, developments of 60 or more dwellings or lots, large shops and offices, large places of assembly and new education centres, including childcare centres, are also referred to DTP. The report needs to suit DTP as well as council.
  • Subdivisions with new roads or intersections: new local roads, intersections and connections to the existing network are usually assessed for layout, capacity and safety.
  • A request for further information: if council's traffic engineer has concerns after lodgement, council can ask for a traffic report under section 54 of the Planning and Environment Act 1987. The request sets a date to respond by, and if it's missed, the application lapses unless council gives more time.

The surest way to know is to ask. Many councils offer pre-application meetings, and the planner or traffic engineer will usually say whether a traffic report is needed and what it should cover.

How detailed the traffic report needs to be

The report should match the proposal. Council wants enough to answer its questions, not a thick document. In practice there are three levels.

  • No traffic report: small proposals with existing access, little extra traffic and parking that meets Clause 52.06. The planner can often assess these from the plans.
  • A short traffic and parking letter: for example, a small multi-unit development or a change of use. It covers the driveway, how far drivers can see when using it (sight distance), whether vehicles can turn in and out (swept paths), parking against Clause 52.06 and the layout against AS 2890.
  • A full traffic impact assessment: for uses that add significant traffic or need access to a main road, such as service stations, supermarkets, childcare centres and larger subdivisions. It adds traffic counts, how many trips the development generates and where they go, and intersection modelling, often in SIDRA, software that tests how intersections cope with the extra traffic.

The Austroads Guide to Traffic Management Part 12: Integrated Transport Assessments for Developments is the standard reference for assessing the traffic impacts of developments. See what's in our traffic impact assessment report.

What council and DTP look for

The people reading the report want to know the development won't create safety or congestion problems they'll have to manage later. That means clear answers to questions like these:

  • Can vehicles enter and leave the site safely, with adequate sight distance?
  • Will queues into or out of the site back onto the road?
  • Do nearby intersections still work at the busiest times?
  • Is there enough parking, and does the layout meet the standards?
  • Can delivery and waste vehicles get in, turn around and leave?
  • Are pedestrians and cyclists catered for at the access and along the frontage?

Clause 65 of the planning scheme also asks council to consider whether loading and unloading facilities are adequate, and their effect on traffic flow and road safety. A report that answers these questions directly, with documented assumptions, is easier to assess.

How to avoid a request for further information

A request for further information on traffic can restart the permit clock and add weeks. Many of council's traffic questions can be answered before you lodge.

  • Ask early: raise traffic at the pre-application meeting so the scope is agreed before work starts.
  • Send the plans you intend to lodge: changes to access, parking or floor area after the report is written mean rework.
  • Tell us how the use will operate: hours, staff numbers, deliveries, waste collection and peak times. They drive the traffic numbers.
  • Show the vehicles that will use the site: include swept paths for delivery and waste trucks, not just cars.
  • Allow time for counts: new traffic counts have to be taken on typical days, outside school and public holidays. At traffic lights, we can often start from the vehicle counts recorded by the signals' own sensors, using Trafficeye, our traffic signal volume tool.
  • Combine reports where you can: access, traffic and parking in one report is usually easier for the assessing planner than separate documents.

Traffic impact assessment or construction traffic plan?

A TIA is about the finished development and the traffic it generates once it's open. Construction traffic is a separate question. Councils and DTP may ask for a construction traffic management plan (CTMP) or a construction traffic impact assessment (CTIA) before works start. These cover truck routes, deliveries, worker parking and any occupation of the road or footpath.

Frequently asked questions

Does every planning application need a traffic impact assessment?

No. Many small applications need only a short letter on access and parking, or no traffic report at all. A full traffic impact assessment is usually needed when a proposal adds significant traffic, changes access to a main road, Council will usually confirm at pre-application. If parking falls short of Clause 52.06, you'll need a car parking demand assessment, which can go in the same report.

Does DTP need to see my traffic report?

It does if the proposal creates or alters access to a road in a Transport Zone 2. Under Clause 52.29 those applications are referred to DTP, which reviews the access and traffic impacts. Larger developments are also referred to DTP under Clause 66.02-11, including 60 or more dwellings or lots, large shops and offices, large places of assembly and new education centres, which include childcare centres.

What happens if council asks for a traffic report after I lodge?

Council can ask for more information under section 54 of the Planning and Environment Act 1987. The request sets a date to respond by. If you miss it, the application lapses, although you can ask council for more time before that date. Send us the request and the plans, and we'll prepare a report that answers each point.

Is a traffic engineering assessment the same thing?

Usually, yes. Councils use different names for the traffic report, including traffic engineering assessment, traffic report and traffic and parking assessment. Check what the request asks the report to cover, rather than the name.

Do I also need a road safety audit?

Sometimes. If the development builds roads or intersections that will be handed over to council, or connects to a DTP road, the permit commonly asks for a road safety audit too. See our guide to when you need a road safety audit.
